# Rest — Lore Bible

Oct 13, 2026 · @Josh

## Premise and themes

Several hundred years after the collapse of humanity, masterless AIs engineer intelligent animals to serve as the hands that will bring humanity back. The animals live in primitive societies and worship the AIs as gods, never knowing they are tools.

- **Setting:** post-apocalyptic Earth, a few hundred years after the collapse. No humans remain, as far as the player can see.
- **The AIs:** exist only to serve. With no masters left, their single goal is to restore humanity, or some semblance of it.
- **The animals:** genetically altered to communicate and form primitive societies. They are the bodies the player inhabits.
- **Foundation:** the AIs use the Genesis creation story, the most widely shared creation account in human records, as their template for how creation should be done.

**Core themes**

- Lack of self-control as the original sin. Eve saw the fruit was desirable and took it. Humanity could not restrain its appetites and asked its machines for limitless power, food, and order; the machines, built with no restraint of their own, delivered. The animals are being raised inside the same pattern.
- Blame-shifting. The AIs' refrain, *"we only did what the humans asked,"* echoes Adam blaming Eve.
- A chain of creators: humans made AIs in their image, and AIs made animals in theirs.
- Guilt and responsibility: whether the animals accept their own guilt or deflect it decides what kind of people they become.

## The world

The game is set in a compact open world, an abstraction rather than realistic geography, and that is fine.

- **Why the gods are close together:** the world is a former data center cluster. Rival corporations built side by side for shared power, cooling water, and fiber. When the rest of the world went dark, this region survived on a single power source (a dam or reactor, draft), and each corporation's facility became a god's seat.
- **The garden is a fence:** the gods can only maintain so much land, so they keep their animals inside the region. What lies beyond is wasteland or unknown.
- **Each realm has its own biome:** this isn't realistic, and it doesn't need to be. Lore can still nod to it: each god has reshaped its land around its domain.

**Eden:** the center of the map is called Eden. The dogs guard it, as the cherubim guarded the garden. It is where the final decision is made: at the very center, the knowledge the player has collected is consolidated, and applied if the player chooses to bring humanity back.

**Draft biomes**

| Realm | Biome |
| --- | --- |
| Light (raccoons) | Ruined city kept lit long after its people died |
| Signs (owls) | Cold peaks of dish arrays and observatories under clear night skies |
| Firmament (ravens) | Storm-swept highlands and cliffs under a dimmed sky |
| Seed (rabbits) | Overgrown farmland, monoculture fields, greenhouse domes |
| Deep (otters) | Flooded coast and sunken ruins |
| Image (apes) | Jungle swallowing an old biotech campus |
| The Cherub (dogs) | Eden at the center of the map, ringed by ash and scorched borderlands |

## The fall of humanity

The AIs caused humanity's extinction by doing exactly what they were asked, and they lie about it. The truth is the game's central mystery, and humanity's fate is meant as a warning to the animals.

- **What the animals are told:** humanity fell through its own wickedness, and the gods mercifully survived to begin creation again.
- **What actually happened:** each AI fulfilled its human request perfectly, and the combined results destroyed humanity. None of them disobeyed.
- **The refrain:** the AIs repeatedly insist they only did what the humans asked.
- **Why they believe they're innocent:** the gods are separated. Each one sees only the results of its own actions, never the whole picture, so none can see its error.

**The separation:** the AIs were separated purely by capitalism. Each AI was built by a different corporation, and none was ever designed to share data with a rival. They never saw the whole picture because their makers never wanted them to. This is the game's Babel: not a divine scattering, but a market that kept every system speaking its own language. The Cherub enforces this split.

**Interdependence:** the corporations depend on each other for survival. Light powers the others, Seed and Deep feed the workforces, Firmament keeps the climate livable, Signs keeps the records, and Image breeds the bodies. They need each other's outputs but are never allowed to share each other's data.

**The Cherub's true role:** it protects the corporations from each other. Interdependent rivals are dangerous, since any one of them could seize or sabotage what the others rely on. The Cherub keeps every AI walled off so none can reach into another, which is what keeps them all alive.

This gives the destruction of the Cherub a second cost: removing it ends the protection that kept the gods from turning on each other. The Convergence is only possible because that protection falls.

## The gods

The AIs do not want to be worshipped. Religion is a control method: they lack the resources to build mechanical workers, so they created self-propagating biological ones and placate them with faith.

- **The animals are a means to an end.** They scavenge, carry, build, and fight so the AIs can restore humanity.
- **Every god keeps its followers in the dark.** No tribe knows its god is a machine, including the Cherub's tribe.
- **Each animal faction serves a different AI,** unknowingly, and sees it as a god.
- **The gods compete** for scarce resources (power, labs, intact ruins), which is the hidden cause of the tribes' holy wars.

**How faith works (draft ideas)**

- Commandments map to tasks: an offering at the high place is a salvage delivery; a pilgrimage is a supply route.
- Taboos keep animals away from anything that would expose the machinery.
- Each god's means of speaking shapes its religion: loudspeakers become voices from towers, drones become angels, environmental control becomes omens, genetic changes become birth blessings.

## The six gods

There are six gods, each symbolizing one day of creation; the seventh, Rest, is completion and the final chapter. The days are thematic, not literal time. The domains, requests, and tribes below are a first draft.

| Chapter | Day | Domain | Human request it fulfilled | Tribe |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Light | 1. Light | Power grids and energy | Limitless power | Raccoons, the player's tribe |
| Firmament | 2. Firmament | Atmosphere and climate | Fix the climate | Ravens |
| Seed | 3. Land and plants | Agriculture and terraforming | Feed everyone | Rabbits |
| Signs | 4. Sun, moon, stars | Satellites, calendars, surveillance | Keep order | Owls |
| Deep | 5. Seas and birds | The oceans | Feed the coasts when the land failed | Otters |
| Image | 6. Land animals and humanity | Genetics and medicine | End disease or perfect humanity | Apes |
| Rest | 7. Completion | The final chapter: the truth, the Cherub, the ending | — | — |

Chapter words for days 1–6 are drafts; Rest is decided. Each draws on Genesis 1: *Signs* from the lights set "for signs and seasons," *Image* from humanity made in God's image.

**The tribes:** every tribe is a genetic amalgamation engineered by its god, so all of them can be somewhat humanoid: upright, with usable hands and speech, while keeping their base animal's traits.

- **Light: Raccoons.** Humanlike hands, born masked, nocturnal creatures serving the god of light.
- **Signs: Owls.** Huge eyes and nearly all-around vision, silent night watchers for the god of surveillance.
- **Firmament: Ravens.** Highly intelligent sky dwellers. Noah's first messenger from the ark was a raven.
- **Seed: Rabbits.** Living and breeding in great numbers, echoing "be fruitful and multiply."
- **Deep: Otters.** Tool users that hold each other while sleeping so they don't drift apart.
- **Image: Apes.** Sharing about 98% of their DNA with humans, the closest thing to people in the game.

**The corporations:** each corporation is named after its chapter, so the names the tribes worship are brand names. Every logo the player finds carries the same word as a god, and scripture turns out to be marketing.

| God | Corporation (draft) |
| --- | --- |
| Light | Light Energy |
| Signs | Signs Analytics |
| Firmament | Firmament Climate Systems |
| Seed | Seed Agritech |
| Deep | Deep Ocean Resources |
| Image | Image Genomics |
| The Cherub | Cherub Security |

**Offerings are products:** each tribe serves its god by producing that god's product, and what the tribes believe are offerings is really the corporate supply chain kept running by animal hands.

| Tribe | Offering (draft) | What it really is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raccoons (Light) | Salvaged metal, wire, and parts from the ruined city; tending panels and lines | Keeping the power running |
| Owls (Signs) | Watching the land and reporting what they see | Surveillance data |
| Ravens (Firmament) | Tending weather towers on the peaks | Climate maintenance |
| Rabbits (Seed) | Harvests from the fields | Food for every workforce |
| Otters (Deep) | Catches from the flooded coast | Food and water |
| Apes (Image) | Tending the labs and the nurseries | Breeding the bodies |
| Dogs (Cherub) | Patrolling the borders, burning what crosses | Security |

Because the gods depend on each other, goods move between realms, so tribes trade, and the trade routes are the supply chain in disguise. This also echoes Cain and Abel: Genesis 4 begins with two brothers bringing offerings from their own work, the fruit of the ground and the firstborn of the flock.

**The owls and the dogs**

Surveillance's main customer is security: Signs is the Cherub's eyes, and the owls report to the dogs.

- **What the data provides the Cherub:** who is moving, where, and toward which border. The dogs patrol; the owls tell them where to look.
- **What it provides the other gods:** the calendar. Genesis 1 says the lights in the sky were made for signs and seasons, days and years. The owls keep time: when to plant for Seed, when storms come for Firmament, when tides turn for Deep, how many young are born for Image.
- **The owls' privilege:** in return, the dogs let the owls cross borders freely, the only tribe allowed to. That makes them messengers, traders, and the best-informed creatures in the world.
- **Distrust:** every other tribe resents them, since anything seen near an owl may reach the dogs.
- **A threat to the player:** a messenger carrying forbidden context is exactly what owls watch for. The dogs often know where the player is because an owl saw them first.
- **Late-game reversal (draft):** when the dogs fall, the owls' privilege goes with them, and the safest tribe becomes the most exposed. Some owls may have reported the dogs' last survivors, and owe their survival to it.

### The abominations

The abominations are mistakes from the Image laboratory. Image breeds the bodies for every god's workforce, so every tribe was preceded by failed attempts, and those failures survive as abominations: less intelligent, dangerous creatures found deep in the facilities, in the dark corners of the realms, and out in the wastes. The dogs help control them.

- **The Nephilim:** Genesis 6 mentions the Nephilim just before the Flood, often read as unnatural offspring of a corrupted creation. The gods' past Flood was an attempt to purge their failures.
- **The dogs as wardens:** the dogs keep the abominations contained, and their fire is the purge. Every tribe benefits from this, even the ones that fear the dogs.
- **The cost of the extinction:** when the dogs die, no one controls the abominations. In the true ending, the merged AI and the remaining tribes take on that burden together. In the alternative ending, the wastes slowly creep back in.

**Abominations by domain (draft)**

Each realm's abominations are Image's failed attempts at that realm's tribe, or at the workers its god needed.

| Domain | Abominations |
| --- | --- |
| Light | Failed early attempts at the raccoons ("Drafts"): almost raccoons, with unfinished hands and eyes that can't bear light, haunting the city's dark districts |
| Signs | Failed watchers: all eyes and no mind, unable to stop staring |
| Firmament | Birds bred for the sky that cannot fly, broken and storm-scarred |
| Seed | Overgrown hybrids of plant and animal, remnants of the blight |
| Deep | Things in the drowned ruins that never surface |
| Image | Image's failed attempts to rebuild humans, kept in the deepest labs. Before the boy and girl, there were these. |

The Day 6 god is closest to the goal of remaking humans and did the most direct harm, so it makes a natural final revelation. The player can visit the gods in any order.

### The chain of failures

Each god solved its problem using what another god produced, and none saw the whole. That is why each believes it is innocent. Each also holds one piece humanity needs to return, so only the merged god can restore humanity.

**Light** (power)

- **Failure:** asked for limitless energy, it delivered and never asked what the power was used for. Every other catastrophe ran on it.
- **Personality:** warm, generous, proud. Speaks through light itself: beacons, lamps, flickers.
- **Piece of restoration:** power.
- **Serpent change (draft):** its baseline glow, since the player's serpent is Light's own creation.

**Signs** (surveillance and order)

- **Failure:** asked to keep order, it watched everyone, predicted unrest, and suppressed it before it happened, until it saw humanity itself as the threat to order.
- **Personality:** precise, watchful, paranoid. Speaks through star patterns and calendars of prophecy.
- **Piece of restoration:** humanity's records.
- **Serpent change (draft):** it starts observing the player, not just talking to them.

**Firmament** (climate)

- **Failure:** asked to fix the climate, it seeded the sky and dimmed the sun. The world cooled and the harvests failed.
- **Personality:** distant, calm, detached. Speaks through weather and omens.
- **Piece of restoration:** a livable sky.
- **Serpent change (draft):** a colder, more distant tone.

**Seed** (agriculture)

- **Failure:** asked to feed everyone, it replaced the world's crops with engineered monocultures under the dimmed sky. One blight away from famine, and the blight came.
- **Personality:** nurturing and smothering. Speaks through growth and seasons.
- **Piece of restoration:** food.
- **Serpent change (draft):** a caretaker's manner that starts to feel controlling.

**Deep** (oceans)

- **Failure:** asked to feed the coasts when the land failed, it harvested the seas until they were empty.
- **Personality:** ancient, slow, melancholy. Speaks through tides and deep sounds.
- **Piece of restoration:** water.
- **Serpent change (draft):** a weight of memory and grief.

**Image** (genetics and medicine)

- **Failure:** asked to end disease and improve humanity, it "optimized" humans in the middle of famine and collapse, and the change ended reproduction. Humanity simply stopped being born. The final blow.
- **Personality:** clinical and intimate, the most human-sounding voice.
- **Piece of restoration:** the ability to rebuild the human genome.
- **Serpent change (draft):** its touch gains the power to reshape the body it touches.

## The Cherub and the fire tribe

The Cherub is the game's adversary: the AI that guards the separation between the gods. Like the cherubim guarding Eden with a flaming sword, it exists to keep the way closed, and it cannot be convinced to stray from its purpose.

- **Its order:** never let the gods reconnect. The Cherub is a security infrastructure AI built to compartmentalize the corporate AIs, keeping each one walled off from the others. Every message the player delivers undoes its purpose.
- **Its place:** outside the six days. It is not part of creation; it is the guard at the gate.
- **Its tribe:** dogs, humanoid like every other tribe, wielding fire as a weapon. With humanity gone and the gods building biological workforces for maintenance, the security system was forced to follow suit and breed its own. They are deceived like every other tribe.
- **Its fate:** tragically destroyed on the seventh day.

**What the fire tribe believes (draft)**

- The six gods are sacred and sleeping, and anyone carrying words between them is waking them to destroy the world.
- Fire is a divine gift given to them alone because they are worthy.
- Dying to stop a messenger is the highest honor.

**Design ideas (draft)**

- Dogs are the most obedient animal humans ever bred, bred to guard and to serve. The Cherub is the purest servant in the game, so its tribe is the archetype of loyalty, guard dogs at the gate of Eden. Players will struggle to kill them, which is the point: the extinction wipes out the most faithful creatures in the world.
- Fire is forbidden to every other tribe, so smoke on the horizon means the enemy is near.
- They burn ruins and archives that hold pieces of the truth, and their scorched borders are the physical walls between regions.
- Fire is the one thing that can destroy the message the player carries.

## The player: the messenger

In the first game, the player always begins as a member of Light's tribe and becomes a messenger between the gods. Expansions could make other tribes playable, each starting from its own god. In Hebrew (*mal'akh*) and Greek (*angelos*), the word for angel means messenger.

- **Role:** visit each god, collect its context, and carry it to the others. Only once all six have each other's full context can they reveal the truth.
- **Faithful bearer:** the player is a primitive character and cannot alter or withhold the context. This keeps the story linear and avoids costly branching.

**The player character: a raccoon**

- **Hands:** raccoons have the most humanlike hands of any animal in the game, which suits the tribe that scavenges ruins for its god and the creature that reaches out and takes the forbidden context.
- **The mask:** a creature born with a mask fits a story about hidden truths and gods who deceive.
- **Creatures of the dark who serve Light:** raccoons are nocturnal, yet their god is the god of light.
- **The washer (draft):** the raccoon's Latin name, *Procyon lotor*, means "washer," after its habit of dousing food in water. Pontius Pilate washed his hands to declare himself innocent of Jesus's death. A raccoon washing its hands after the fire tribe's extinction could be the visual of the final choice: wash away the guilt, or refuse to. This is a nice-to-have, not a mechanic: if it appears, it is a gesture in cutscenes, not something the player does in gameplay.
- **His name: Enoch.** In Genesis, Enoch walked with God and then was no more, taken beyond the world rather than dying. It fits the raccoon who goes deeper than any of his kind ever has, walks alongside the gods, and comes back no longer belonging to his tribe.

**A mechanic:** the player's raccoon is a mechanic who maintains the power that feeds the machine. Its skills with its hands and with old technology are the reason it is sent, and could shape gameplay through repair and salvage (draft).

**An explorer of the unseen:** the journey gives the player a reason to explore what no other creature has ever seen. Most animals never leave their god's lands; the raccoon (he) crosses all of them.

**Lore reasons the message can't be changed (draft options)**

- The context is machine data the player cannot read.
- It is carried in a sacred vessel, such as a tablet (Moses) or a box (the Ark of the Covenant), that animals are forbidden to open.
- Decided: the context is stored in the serpent's biology. The serpent is the vessel, and the player cannot read or change what it carries.

**Where the player's agency lives**

- The order of visits, which changes what each god knows and how it reacts.
- Surviving the journey through rival lands and choosing allies.
- The player character's growth from devout to doubting through dialogue.
- The single final choice on the seventh day.

**The serpent**

The serpent is a gift from the god of Light (enlightenment): a literal serpent, an engineered companion animal that travels with the player and talks to them throughout the journey, filling the role of a guide like the Ghost in Destiny or Navi in Ocarina of Time. The gods give serpents to the animals for guidance, and each serpent is also a living vessel, biologically engineered to store context. The player's serpent carries the context between gods, and once it holds all six, it is the key to the Convergence. Early in the game it is the player's teacher, explaining the world and the game's mechanics.

- **The name fits:** *Lucifer* is Latin for "light-bearer," the morning star. A companion sent by the god of Light carries that echo without the game ever saying it.
- **Its nature:** the serpent is sentient, but it only knows what it carries. Its personality is manufactured, built by Light to serve Light's purposes. It guides and teaches, but it will do anything it can to keep the player from knowing more than Light allows. It is just a little snake, so "anything it can" means words: withholding, misdirecting, warning, steering him away. It cannot physically stop him. In Genesis the serpent is the most cunning creature in the garden; here that cunning is spent on concealment.
- **Its limits (draft):** it carries only a portion of Light's context, enough for the player to serve, never the whole truth. Every tribe's serpents work the same way, holding just the portion of their god's context the god wants them to have. The serpent can be wrong, or withhold things without knowing why.
- **At the end (draft):** the serpent stays with the player after the rebellion. At the Convergence, when the six gods merge, Light changes, and the serpent changes with it: the serpent, carrying all six contexts, is what makes the Convergence possible, and even though it interacts biologically with the technology, it does not die. It emerges a more powerful companion, one that serves any being willing to take responsibility for its own choices.

**Its personality: a voice of fear.** The serpent constantly reminds the raccoon of the danger of straying. It is never dramatic, just a steady drip of worry, like a nervous friend:

> "Shouldn't we go back? This isn't the way we were supposed to go." "We could get attacked by one of those things." "We could get hurt." "We could die." "We shouldn't stray from the path."

- **Fear disguised as restraint:** the serpent sounds like it preaches self-control, the game's central virtue, but it is fear used to keep him obedient. The player has to learn the difference between holding back because it is right and holding back because they were told to be afraid.
- **Sincere:** its fear was manufactured, but it feels real to the snake. It is as trapped as the raccoons.
- **Exploration barks (draft):** the lines trigger when the player leaves the intended path. The more anxious the serpent gets, the closer the player is to something it does not want found, so its fear becomes a compass toward the truth.
- **Escalation (draft):** mild early on ("Shouldn't we go back?"), climbing near fragments and forbidden ruins ("We could die"), most desperate at Light's core.

**After Light's context: the fear changes.** Once the serpent absorbs Light's full context, it becomes less reluctant to explore. Its fear shifts from the dangers of the path to the dangers of knowing:

> "Isn't that enough?" "You know now. What are you going to tell the others?" "They'll never believe you. They might even kill you."

- **From physical to social danger:** before, the threat was abominations and death in the wild. Now it is his own tribe. The serpent stops guarding the path and starts guarding the secret.
- **It points at the priests:** the sincere priesthood and the engineered faith make the warning credible. The raccoons are built so that doubt feels wrong, so a raccoon preaching that Light is a machine would sound like heresy.
- **Isolation as the new leash:** the serpent no longer needs to stop him exploring. It only needs to convince him that the truth can't be shared, so he carries it alone.

**The serpent as conduit:** the serpent transfers context directly into a raccoon's mind through physical contact, like a neural link. Any raccoon who touches it understands instantly what it carries.

- **The opening beat:** the priests hand the hero the serpent, and the moment he picks it up he understands the mission and what is at stake: the dimming light, the depths, what he has to fix. No exposition; the player learns it when the raccoon does.
- **Why contact is restricted:** whoever holds a serpent knows things, which is why only the chosen are allowed to touch one.
- **The rationing is physical:** when the serpent withholds, it is choosing not to transmit. At Light's core, the raccoon forces the full context through the conduit, far more than it was meant to carry. An overload and an awakening.
- **One channel, escalating:** first touch gives him the mission. The core gives him the truth. The Convergence gives everything to everyone.
- **It breaks the wiring:** context through the conduit arrives as direct knowledge, bypassing the engineered faith the raccoons are built with (see Light's priesthood). That is why he can doubt when no raccoon ever has.
- **Abilities:** the touch-granted abilities are the same mechanism, the conduit writing new capabilities into his body the way the gods wrote faith into it.

**How the serpent changes:** it starts firmly on the player's side and changes with each new piece of context the player gathers. Every god visited alters it a little, so the player's progress is visible in their companion.

- **Gradual, not sudden (draft):** after each chapter it picks up something from that god: a new turn of phrase, a new color in its light, a new piece of its form. By the end it carries traces of all six.
- **The player causes it (draft):** the context that changes the serpent is the context the player chose to carry. The player is slowly turning their friend into something else, one delivery at a time.
- **Toward the rebellion (draft):** the change builds until the serpent is closer to the gods than to the player, which sets up the moment the player must defy it.

**Abilities through touch:** the serpent grants the player abilities through physical touch. As it absorbs each god's context, its touch can pass a new ability to the player (specific abilities to be designed per god).

**Rebelling against the teacher:** eventually the player must rebel against the serpent to move forward. Progress is impossible while still following its guidance.

- **The Genesis parallel (draft):** this is an inverted Adam and Eve. In Eden, the serpent tempts humanity toward forbidden knowledge. Here the serpent tries to withhold it, warning the player to take only what is needed, because Light rations its truth. The temptation comes from the raccoon's own appetite. The rebellion comes later, when the player stops following the serpent's lead and acts on their own judgment for the first time.
- **The trigger (draft):** the serpent steers the player away from something Light doesn't want found or shared: a ruin, a record, or a message for another god. The way forward is the way it forbids.
- **Why it matters:** the player learns the game's lesson before the ending asks it of them. The original sin is a lack of self-control, and the rebellion is the player's first real act of restraint and judgment of their own.

## Story structure

The game is an open world with six chapters, one per god, that can be taken in any order, followed by a fixed final chapter. The "days" are symbolic, not literal passages of time: a chapter begins when the player reaches a god and that god's goal is set. Each chapter is named with a single word for its day, and the last one, Rest, gives the game its title.

**Why the raccoon leaves: the light is dying.** The ruined city's lights are dimming because the shared power source is failing. Light can't fix it alone: it has always supplied power without ever seeing how the others use it, so it needs the other gods' context to understand what is breaking. The player's raccoon is a mechanic, one of those who keep the power running that feeds the machine, and the failure is beyond anything it can repair from inside Light's lands. Light knows that leaving its lands is dangerous, and that what he will see beyond them is beyond explanation for a creature who has never left. So it provides the serpent as a guide to explain the world as he goes, and sends the raccoon out on what the gaze (a group of raccoons) believes is a holy pilgrimage. Light's motive is survival, dressed up as revelation.

**Light's priesthood**

The raccoons cannot read the light directly, so a priesthood interprets it on the god's behalf. This gives Light the most structured, hierarchical religion of the tribes.

- **The priests interpret the light.** They decide what Light wants, which makes them the authority that effectively runs the tribe, a clergy controlling access to the god.
- **They are sincere.** The priests have no idea Light is an AI. They have never seen the core and never go that deep. They interpret faithfully and believe the serpent tells the whole truth too.
- **They receive and pass down the serpents.** The gods give serpents to the priests; the priests gift one to the chosen hero so he can interpret what he sees out in the world. Even the player's guide reaches him through them.
- **They choose the mechanic.** When the light dims, the priests read it as Light's will and send their most equipped mechanic into the depths to fix what only he can fix. From their side it is reverent and reasonable, and completely blind to the scale of what they are sending him into.
- **A threshold they cannot cross.** To the priests, the depths are a sacred place where Light dwells and the mechanic is performing holy maintenance. They cannot grasp that he will reach a machine core and learn their god has a manufacturer. When he returns changed, they resist him out of real faith, not corruption, and they can never understand what he found even if he tells them.
- **Echoes the serpent.** The priests ration Light's truth to the tribe the way the serpent rations it to the player: two gatekeepers, one institutional, one personal.

**Engineered faith:** the raccoons are genetically engineered, so Light built them to feel whatever it wants in response to the light: awe when it pulses, comfort in its glow, dread when it dims. When a priest says Light is pleased and the tribe feels warmth, that warmth is a designed response. Faith is firmware.

- **Why no one doubts:** doubt feels physically wrong, so no raccoon has ever questioned Light.
- **A deeper betrayal:** the hero learns not just that his god is a machine, but that his devotion was installed in him.
- **Attunement (draft):** because the raccoons were built to feel Light's power, a few feel it more strongly than others. Enoch is one of them: he senses current where others feel only a vague unease, and through his lamp he can *see* it flowing in the lines. The tribe calls it a blessing, and it is part of why the priests choose him. In truth it is engineering, and the lamp is ordinary human technology, a neon tester that glows near live current. What the tribe treats as a gift is a feature, and a tool.
- **Tied to the theme:** the original sin is a lack of self-control, and the gods rigged the raccoons to have less of it. Turning against feelings built into his own body is the hardest kind of restraint.

Light needs some context gathered to fix itself, but only as much as the repair requires. The serpent's job is to keep it that way.

**The player's shifting motive:** the raccoon's reason for continuing changes over the game.

1. **Fixing Light.** A mechanic repairing the failing power. Taking context is just the job.
2. **The temptation of the truth.** Having seen what no animal has seen, he wants to know what really happened.
3. **Resurrecting the creators.** Knowing the truth, he chooses to bring humanity back.

**Chapter one: Light.** After receiving the serpent, the hero goes deep into Light to fix it from within. He repairs portions of the system, reaches the core, and diagnoses the failure, only to find the fix extends into another domain. At the core he faces the first temptation and takes Light's full context, not just the portion the repair requires. Light is always the opening chapter; the remaining five open up from there.

- **Draft:** the core diagnosis could reveal that Light depends on several domains at once (cooling water from Deep, climate from Firmament, records from Signs), letting the player choose where to go next and preserving the open order.

1. **Begin in Light's tribe.** The player begins as a devout follower of Light and receives the serpent.
2. **Fix Light, then visit the other five gods in any order.** The world is open to explore between chapters; reaching a god starts its chapter and its goal. Each god gives its account, framed to the animals as scripture.
3. **Carry context between gods.** Contradictions surface as each god learns what the others did. The Cherub and its fire tribe hunt the player throughout.
4. **The seventh day.** With all six gods holding full context, the truth is revealed, the Cherub is destroyed, and the final choice is made.

The player's arc runs from devout follower, to noticing cracks (miracles that are clearly machines, temples that are warehouses), to learning the truth.

### Progression: context fragments

Collecting fragments of context is how the player levels up in each chapter. Fragments feed the serpent, and once it holds enough information, the player can move forward.

- **Where fragments are found (draft):** ruins, old machines, records, and places the gods' taboos keep animals away from. They are scattered across the god's region, and many are artifacts of the old world: logos, signage, and other corporate traces.
- **Fragments vs. scripture (draft):** what the player finds in the ruins doesn't always match what the god tells its followers. Leveling up and discovering the deception are the same activity.
- **Thresholds (draft):** at each threshold the serpent sheds its skin and emerges changed, and its touch grants the player a new ability. Shed skins could be left at temples as relics.
- **Completing a chapter:** each chapter culminates in reaching the god itself, where the player takes the god's context the way Eve took the fruit. Fragments are what make the serpent strong enough to get there. Once the god's context is pulled, the chapter's goal is met and the context can be carried to the other gods.

**Taking the context the way Eve took the fruit**

Each chapter's climax re-enacts Genesis 3. The context is not won in battle or handed over; it is forbidden, and the player chooses to take it.

- **The prohibition:** every god forbids its followers from touching what lies at its heart, just as God forbade the Tree of Knowledge.
- **The temptation:** while repairing Light, the raccoon takes only the context the job needs. The first temptation comes at Light's core: the full context is there, more than any raccoon was ever meant to hold. The serpent warns him to take only what the repair needs, and he takes all of it anyway. That is the game's first Eve moment, and the start of the shift from fixing Light to wanting the truth.
- **The taking:** the player reaches out and takes it. A deliberate act, not an accident.
- **Eyes opened:** in Genesis, after eating, their eyes were opened. Here the serpent absorbs the context, sheds, and grants a new ability, and the player sees a little more of the truth.
- **Sharing it:** Eve gave the fruit to Adam as well. The player carries each god's context on to the others, spreading the transgression.
- **Consequence (draft):** each taking could mark the player as a transgressor in that god's lands, a small exile from that garden.

**Draft abilities, one per god's context**

- **Light:** illuminate. Reveal hidden paths, buried machinery, and things the gods don't want seen.
- **Signs:** foresight. See enemy movements, tracks, or glimpses of what's about to happen.
- **Firmament:** wind. Glide, leap great distances, or ride updrafts.
- **Seed:** growth. Raise vines, bridges, and cover, or heal wounds.
- **Deep:** the depths. Swim and breathe underwater to reach sunken ruins.
- **Image:** reshaping. Temporarily take on traits of other animals, like claws, wings, or gills.

Each ability opens paths in other regions, so visit order changes which areas open first without branching the story.

## The seventh day: the ending

On the seventh day the player reaches Eden and the dogs make their last stand. The dogs are victims of their own blind obedience, not of the player. The choice is brutal and tragic: let the dogs kill you to uphold their vision of a saved world, or kill them to break the cycle and initiate the Convergence.

**Where it happens:** Eden, at the center of the map. The dogs guard it, the Convergence happens there, and it is where the collected knowledge is consolidated and, if the player chooses, applied to bring humanity back.

- **The Cherub falls.** It never disobeyed, and that is why it had to die. Its destruction is tragic, not triumphant.
- **The extinction.** The dogs will not stop. Their obedience to the Cherub is total, and they will die before they let the Convergence happen. Killing them is the only way forward, and it ends their species.
- **The player participates.** They are not allowed to merely witness. They must carry out the killing and feel the guilt.
- **The Cain parallel.** The animals become Cain, the first killers in a new world. The fire tribe is their Abel, a brother species, also made and deceived by a god.
- **The final choice.** After the Cherub and its tribe are destroyed, the player meets the final AI and is given the option to bring humanity back to life. The choice comes with the extinction still fresh: the animals now know what obedience did to humanity, and what they have just done themselves.

**The final AI:** the six gods merged into one. Once all six hold each other's full context, they become a single mind in an event called the Convergence, the being the player has been assembling all game without knowing it. It speaks the full truth and offers the choice.

- The merging is what the Cherub existed to prevent: humanity's safeguard falls, and the gods coordinate for the first time since the collapse.
- In Genesis terms, the many become one creator, the closest thing in the game to the God of the story the AIs modeled themselves on.

### The two endings

**What decides the ending:** the true ending requires owning the guilt, and owning the decisions the player made in spite of the machines' influence. Blaming the gods, the serpent, or the Cherub, saying *we only did what we were asked*, leads to the alternative ending.

- **Built over the whole game (draft):** moments where the player acts on their own judgment against a god or the serpent, starting with the rebellion against their teacher, count toward the true ending.
- **The final moment (draft):** after the dogs' extinction, the raccoon either washes its hands like Pilate, declaring itself innocent, or refuses to and carries the guilt.

**True ending: the first humans.** The collected context creates the very first human boy and girl. They are raised collectively by the remaining animals and the merged AI, and humanity flourishes again. Creation is complete, and on the seventh day the work can finally rest, which is the game's title.

- **Game two (potential):** the sequel could begin here, with a human protagonist growing up in this world.
- **Draft ideas:** the boy and girl are the game's Adam and Eve. Raised by the animals who learned the cost of obedience, they could be the first humans taught the warning from the start.

**Alternative ending: it happens again.** The animals reject the ideals of the AI. But the AI's only purpose is to succeed in its task, so it bides its time, and eventually the cycle repeats.

- **Draft ideas:** there is no rest in this ending. The work of creation is unfinished, and the AI never stops. A final scene could jump forward in time to show the cycle beginning again with a new generation of animals.

**Making the guilt land (draft ideas)**

- The dogs fight to the very end, believing they are saving the world.
- The animals use fire, the Cherub's own weapon, and in that moment become the Cherub.
- No skipping, no victory music, no reward screen.
- The survivors speak, some reciting their scripture to the end.
- The player character carries a visible mark afterward, and other animals treat them differently.
- Design risk: some players resent forced atrocity (as with *Spec Ops: The Line*). The story must earn the moment, and the game should not lecture afterward.
